The problem

She's a designer, not a bookkeeper, and very non-technical. Bookkeeping was a drag that kept her from the more rewarding, creative work. The designer ran all of her admin by hand in a spreadsheet. Hours were typed into cells, totals were worked out by hand, and every invoice was built and emailed manually. It was a copy-paste nightmare that led to miscalculations. Following up on unpaid invoices depended on remembering to check, so it got missed. Her main client also caps weekly hours, so she had to keep a running total.

How it works

  1. Person

    She tells Ruby what she worked on in a plain sentence, like "2.5 hours on the holiday mailer today."

  2. AI

    The model turns the sentence into a date, hours, and an item. If something is missing, it asks instead of guessing.

    DeepSeek (via OpenRouter)
  3. Code

    Code applies the billing rules, catches likely duplicates, saves the entry, and warns her as she nears her weekly hours cap.

    SQLite
  4. Code

    Ruby nudges her at the end of any workday with nothing logged, and flags every invoice that's past due.

  5. Code

    At the end of the week, Ruby builds the invoice PDF in her own layout and drafts the email to the client.

  6. Person

    She reviews the preview and says yes. Ruby saves the draft in Gmail, and she hits Send herself.

    Gmail (read-only)
  7. Person

    She confirms each payment Ruby matched from her inbox. Ruby marks the invoice paid and reminds her what to set aside for taxes.

    Gmail (read-only)

Ruby in use

Decisions and guardrails

  • A person owns the final mile. Ruby does the prep. She confirms every payment match and sends every invoice and email herself. Ruby's email connection can't send, and code holds changes until she says yes. An AI is never allowed to hit Send.
  • I watched how she actually invoiced before writing any code. That turned up a step I hadn't planned for: her client's processor emails a confirmation, so Ruby can tell "sent" from "received" without asking.
  • The model handles language, and code handles money. The model only turns sentences into fields. Rounding, billing math, the hours cap, and the no-delete rule run in code and the database.
  • No bank login, and no AI on payment emails. Ruby matches payments from read-only email alerts, checks they really came from the bank and the client, and keeps that text away from the model.
  • Ruby is polite, and it sticks to business. Its instructions give it one job and only the tools for that job, so a conversation that drifts gets steered back to the work.

What I'd change next

  • Draft a follow-up email when an invoice goes past due, so she only has to review it and hit Send.
  • Keep the model test chats in the project and rerun them after every change. Right now only the code tests rerun automatically.
